Expired Orders Create "Phantom Liquidity," Allowing Mark Price Manipulation and Liquidation Evasion

## Summary
The functions used to calculate the order book's impact price, a critical component of the `markPrice`, fail to check if resting orders have expired. This allows an attacker to create a fake wall of liquidity using orders with a short expiry. After these orders expire, they remain as "ghosts" that are still counted by the impact price simulation. This allows the attacker to artificially inflate or deflate the `markPrice`, preventing their own legitimate liquidations and putting the protocol's solvency at risk.

## Finding Description
The protocol's `markPrice` is calculated as the median of three components: `p1` (oracle price), `p2` (EMA of basis spread), and `p3` (TWAP of impact price). The `impactPrice` is meant to be a measure of the "real" price after accounting for slippage on the on-chain order book.

The calculation of this `impactPrice` is performed by the `getImpactPrice` function, which in turn calls simulation functions like `quoteAskInQuote` to determine the book's depth. The vulnerability lies deep within this simulation, in the helper functions that read the order book data.

The function `_getQuoteLimit` (and its counterpart `_getBaseLimit`) is responsible for iterating through the orders at a specific price level to calculate the available liquidity. However, it is missing a crucial check.

**File**: `contracts/perps/types/Book.sol`
Because this check is missing, the `getImpactPrice` function operates on corrupted data. It treats expired, invalid orders as real, tradeable liquidity.

**The Attack Path:**
1.  **The Setup:** An attacker with a large `long` position places a wall of large `BUY` orders with a short expiry time (e.g., 60 seconds) at prices far below the current market.
2.  **The Orders Expire:** After 60 seconds, these orders are now "ghosts." The real matching engine would ignore them, but the `_getQuoteLimit` function does not.
3.  **The Market Crashes:** The real market price (`indexPrice`) falls sharply, putting the attacker's `long` position at risk of liquidation.
4.  **The Manipulation:** The protocol calls `setMarkPrice`. This triggers a call to `getImpactPrice`, which simulates a large sell to find the `impactBid`. The simulation function (`_getQuoteLimit`) encounters the attacker's wall of expired ghost orders and incorrectly reports that there is massive buy-side liquidity. This results in an artificially **high** `impactBid`.
5.  **The Result:** This artificially high `impactBid` becomes `p3` in the median calculation, pulling the final `markPrice` upwards and preventing it from reflecting the real, crashing market price. The attacker's liquidation is wrongfully avoided.

## Impact Explanation
This is a **High Severity** vulnerability that undermines the core security of the protocol.

*   **Direct Manipulation of Mark Price:** It allows a malicious user to directly influence the `markPrice`, which is the source of truth for all PnL and liquidation calculations.
*   **Liquidation Evasion & Protocol Insolvency:** An attacker can prevent their own legitimate liquidation. This forces the protocol to carry a toxic, insolvent position for longer than it should, increasing the risk of massive bad debt when the position is eventually closed at a much worse price. This bad debt drains the Insurance Fund and threatens protocol solvency.
*   **Broken Risk Management:** The `impactPrice` is designed to be a safety mechanism that makes the `markPrice` more resilient. This bug turns it into a vector for attack, completely defeating its purpose.

## Likelihood Explanation
The likelihood is **Medium**. The attack does not require a large amount of capital (as the manipulative orders can be placed far from the market price) but does require a specific market condition (a sharp price move that threatens the attacker's position). The attack is subtle and requires a good understanding of the protocol's mechanics, but it is a clear and profitable strategy for a malicious actor.
