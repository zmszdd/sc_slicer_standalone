Mint Cap Bypass in Small Markets Allows Catastrophic Market Takeover

## Finding description and impact

* A serious logic error in the `_checkMintCap` function allows attackers to bypass mint limits in small markets by exploiting an incorrect AND operator. This enables minting astronomical amounts (up to 2^96 tokens) in markets that should be protected, leading to complete market takeover and economic destruction.


* The `_checkMintCap` function in `LatentSwapLogic.sol` contains a fundamental logic flaw that completely undermines the protocol's safety mechanisms for small markets.

Vulnerable Code (Lines 1066-1069):

The issue lies in the use of the AND operator (`&&`) instead of OR (`||`). According to the comments on lines 1057-1058, the intended behavior is to allow minting "up to 2^96" in small markets. However, the current logic creates a bypass condition.

When an attacker mints exactly `FixedPoint.Q96` (2^96) tokens:
- `(mintAmount > FixedPoint.Q96)` evaluates to `(2^96 > 2^96)` = **false**
- `(mintAmount > marketBaseTokenSupply)` evaluates to `(2^96 > small_amount)` = **true**
- `false && true` = **false** → No revert occurs

This allows the attacker to mint exactly 2^96 tokens, which is approximately 79,228,162,514,264,337,593,543,950,336  an astronomical amount that completely breaks the market economics.

The developers intended to prevent mints that exceed BOTH the Q96 limit AND the market size. However, the AND condition creates a loophole where minting exactly Q96 bypasses both checks. The correct logic should use OR to ensure mints are capped at the MINIMUM of Q96 or marketBaseTokenSupply.


* This security issue has catastrophic consequences for protocol integrity. An attacker can completely take over any small market with a single transaction, gaining near-total ownership (99%+) of all synthetic tokens. This breaks the fundamental economic model where markets should have distributed ownership and fair price discovery. The attack dilutes existing holders to near-zero value while giving the attacker control over market pricing and future liquidity. Since small markets are common during protocol launch or when new assets are added, this represents a systemic threat that could be exploited repeatedly across multiple markets, effectively allowing attackers to monopolize entire asset categories within the protocol.
